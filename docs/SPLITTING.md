# Splitting Rules — World Plan Structure

Reference for how the world plan is organized into complexes, how production
localizes vs ships, and in what order things get built. Applied uniformly to
every part of the plan; nothing is a special case.

## Why this exists

The plan was generated by a world solve that summed the whole map's demand for
each item and dropped the totals into functional buckets. The math is right, but
it produced a handful of mega-complexes each making the entire world's supply of
everything — the megafactory pattern. Community practice and the official wiki
both favor the opposite: many smaller, distributed complexes, each making a tight
family near its resource or its consumer, linked by logistics. Distributing is
easier to reorganize, easier on game performance, and — with the alt recipes this
save uses — usually cheaper to transport. These rules turn the flat solve into
that distributed graph.

## The model

**One node type: the complex.** A complex is a production grouping tied to a
site — a real cluster of nodes, of any size (a single node up to a sprawl of
many), possibly holding several different resources. There is no separate
"sub-complex" type. Whether a complex acts as a supplier or a distributor is read
off its output edges, not stamped on the complex as a name.

**Edges are the outputs.** Every part a complex produces leaves on an edge to one
of three destinations:

- a **named complex** (a dedicated feed — this complex supplies that one),
- the **depot**, or
- the **world / map** (general distribution).

An edge is flagged as either a **dedicated feed** (belt, truck, or train straight
to one named consumer) or a **distribution drop** (to depot or world). That flag
drives the volume rule and the transport view.

**The graph.** Edges form a directed graph of any depth: a complex can feed
another complex that feeds another, and a complex can feed several parents at
once. The graph converges on the final-assembly complexes that make the
space-elevator parts. "Supplier" and "distributor" are just descriptions of which
way a complex's edges point — a complex can be both at once, supplying one part to
a builder while dropping another to the depot.

**What is not a complex.** A freight-consolidation point — where several streams
bundle onto one shared train so you don't run ten half-empty ones — is a logistics
station, not a complex. It carries no production and wears no producer label.

**No pass-through.** If a complex would take a part in and ship it out unchanged,
that complex should not exist. That is just the upstream complex shipping to the
world; delete the middle hop.

**Physical realization is a note, not structure.** Whether a complex is its own
building, a floor, or a roped-off division inside a larger building is a
build-time property. The plan carries the logical graph; the physical arrangement
is a note on it.

## The one rule: localize vs ship

Every "make it here or ship it in" decision uses a single test, applied at every
step of every chain:

> Weigh producing locally (or pulling it in on a dedicated feed) against shipping
> the finished part — with each item weighted by its recipe's **volume
> multiplier**. Keep producing locally while the inputs plus feeds are cheaper to
> move than the finished part. Ship the finished part only when it is denser to
> move than everything that feeds it.

The volume multiplier is what makes this concrete, and the alt recipes flip the
usual intuition:

- **Pure Iron Ingot** is ~2x the volume of the ore that feeds it; **Pure Copper
  Ingot** ~2.5x. With the Pure recipes this save uses, an ingot stream is *more*
  expensive to move than its ore — so smelting happens on the node, and ingots are
  not shipped raw across the map.
- **Fluids** (acids, alumina, water) can't enter the depot and are pipe-throughput
  limited, so they stay local by default.

**Co-location comes first.** When several needed resources sit on one site, one
complex claims them all and processes locally. This isn't just convenience — the
two-ore alt recipes only work co-located: **Copper Alloy Ingot** needs copper
*and* iron ore together (halving copper draw), **Basic Iron Ingot** needs iron +
limestone, and the iron/copper alloys likewise. Co-location is what makes the
recipe strategy physically real, with zero ore shipped.

**When nodes aren't co-located, split and feed — volume picks the direction.**
Process the denser-to-move item on its own node and pull the cheaper one toward
it. Copper (~2.5x) is expensive once it's ingot, so copper is processed on its
node and iron is shipped in, rather than both going to a neutral third site.

## Precedence: geography beats volume

The volume rule optimizes, but it does not override physical reality. **Geography
and manageability are hard constraints; the volume rule works within them.** If a
node cluster can't host a step, the volume rule doesn't get to demand it there — it
just picks the least-bad ship. Likewise, a chain may be split into separate
complexes purely to stay manageable or to fit a region, even when volume alone
wouldn't require it; in that case the volume rule only decides whether the link
between them needs a dedicated line or can be a simple drop.

## Granularity

Group by **site and resource family plus immediate derivatives** — not one item
per complex, and not everything in one place. A site does its extraction and every
processing step whose inputs are all local — ingots, steel where coal is local,
concrete, copper alloy — and hands off once a step needs inputs from elsewhere
(which becomes another edge in the graph, not a reason to force-fit the step
locally). Follow the chain; stop where the local inputs stop. How finely a long
chain is broken into separate complexes (ingots here, next-tier parts there) is
chosen to keep each piece manageable and to fit the region — a logical split, with
the volume rule only deciding whether the link between the pieces is a dedicated
feed or a drop.

## Build order

Phase-gated on power and tier. No complex is built before the producers of its
inputs exist, and power always leads demand.

1. **Black-start coal** on an isolated switch — built first; the only thing that
   can restart a dead grid.
2. **Foundation bus** — iron, concrete, basic parts.
3. **Fuel-power bridge** — powers all construction and remains the backup grid.
4. **Mid-tier** — oil & polymer, aluminium, electronics, motors, frames.
5. **Nuclear** — waste reprocessing live *before* the reactors start, to hold the
   zero-waste invariant.
6. **Endgame** — SAM, quantum, project assembly → space elevator.

The build-step numbering (1.1, 1.2, …) encodes this order on each complex.

## Worked examples

**Multi-parent supplier — copper + limestone on one site.** A site sits on copper
and limestone. It processes both locally, then sends limestone (or concrete) to a
steel complex for encased beams and copper (as alloy ingot or wire) to an
electronics complex — two dedicated feeds from one complex to two different
parents. It's a pure supplier: both outputs land on named builders, neither on the
world. Nothing about it needs a second label; the two edges say everything.

**Split-and-feed — iron and copper not co-located.** Ideal case: a site holds
both, so one complex runs Copper Alloy Ingot and ships nothing raw. If they're
apart: copper is the denser move once ingot (~2.5x), so a complex sits on the
copper node and processes it there, while iron ore is shipped in to feed the alloy
step — move the cheaper-to-transport ore toward the more-expensive one, rather than
hauling both to a neutral site.

## What the deliverable becomes

Applying this dissolves the flat list of solver complexes into a **graph of
site-anchored complexes** connected by typed edges. The node map, the
100%-utilization accounting, and the per-complex views all speak that graph rather
than a flat list. The flat set was always a staging point, not the shape of the
answer.
